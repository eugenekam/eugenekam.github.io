I’m not a Formula 1 analyst yet, and I won’t pretend to be. What I have done since 2019 is the job underneath it: take a system that’s supposed to be working, find where it’s losing time or money, and prove it with the data.

Where did 0.054 go?

Singapore, 10 October 2026. Max Verstappen took pole from Charles Leclerc by 0.054 seconds. After 4.9 km that’s 4 metres, less than the length of a car.

Leclerc led with one sector left

Verstappen’s time came on his first Q3 run and his second didn’t beat it, so Leclerc’s final run was his last shot at it. His middle sector put him 0.107 ahead with about 26 seconds to go. Through that sector he was quicker than Verstappen at five of the seven corners and slower at none.

Then he lost 0.161 in sector three

Verstappen’s 25.840 was the fastest third sector of the whole session, by anyone. Through it he was quicker at four of the six corners. Leclerc’s two wins there were a single km/h each.

The same choice, two results

At Turn 7 and Turn 16 Leclerc dropped to second gear where Verstappen stayed in third. At Turn 7 he carried 5 km/h more through the apex. At Turn 16, in the sector that decided pole, he carried 6 km/h less. The buttons above go straight to both.

How far this can be trusted: times at the three timing lines are official. Between them the public feed has about four to six samples a second. That’s enough to read apex speeds and gears, but too coarse to put a time on a single corner or to compare braking points, so nothing here does. The delta line is a broad trend pinned to the official splits, and the feed puts both cars on one line, so the 3D cars run a lane apart to stay visible.
